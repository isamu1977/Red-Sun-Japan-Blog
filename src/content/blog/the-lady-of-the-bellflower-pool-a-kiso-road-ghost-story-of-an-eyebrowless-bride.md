Snow melts against a lamp-oil wick, and a woman sits before a standing mirror drawing color along her lower lip with the patience of someone who has outlived urgency. She lifts a folded paper to her brow as though to conceal something, then lowers it. Beneath the powder there is only smooth, blue-white skin where eyebrows ought to be. She turns, and asks the traveler beneath the bear-pelt quilt a single question: *niau ka* — does it suit me? Before he can answer, the ceiling bends to receive her, and the room is no longer a room.

This is **the Lady of the Bellflower Pool**, a Kiso Road ghost story of an eyebrowless bride that survives today not as folklore passed by firelight but as a piece of literary *kaidan* — a travel account attributed to **Izumi Kyōka**, the Meiji-era master of Japanese gothic prose, preserved in Japan's **Aozora Bunko** digital archive. It is a tale built from precise geography, real custom, and the quiet cruelty that so often outlives the people who commit it.

## The Inn Beneath the Thatched Eave

![Soba Supper at Inn](/images/posts/the-lady-of-the-bellflower-pool-a-kiso-road-ghost-story-of-an-eyebrowless-bride/shot_012.jpeg)
*Soba Supper at Inn.*


The account is anchored to **Narai-juku**, an actual post station on the old **Nakasendō** highway through the Kiso Valley of Nagano Prefecture, roughly three thousand two hundred feet above sea level and one hundred fifty-eight miles from the rail terminus at Iidamachi. The narrator relays testimony gathered from a traveling companion, **Sakai Sankichi**, who passed through the valley during *shimotsuki*, the eleventh month the old calendar named for frost.

Narai was, and remains, one of the better-preserved way stations of the Kiso road, its eaves low, its timber dark with two centuries of hearth smoke. Sankichi had not intended to stop there at all. His ticket read Agematsu, farther down the valley, where the ivy-hung plank bridges and the sleeping ledge of the Kiso River drew sightseers in those years. Hunger, not design, delivered him to Narai — and hunger, as the story makes plain, has its own sense of irony along this particular road.

## Two Bowls of Soba: The Shadow of Jippensha Ikku

![Hearth Fire Tender](/images/posts/the-lady-of-the-bellflower-pool-a-kiso-road-ghost-story-of-an-eyebrowless-bride/shot_023.jpeg)
*Hearth Fire Tender.*


Before Sankichi ever reached Narai, the tale pauses to invoke an older, comic ghost of the same highway: **Yajirōbei and Kitahachi**, the bumbling travelers of **Jippensha Ikku's** *Zoku Hizakurige*, the Kiso Road sequel to his famous Edo-period comic travelogue. A generation earlier the pair crossed Torii Pass and haggled their way into a cheap inn for a meager supper of buckwheat noodles, only to be sent to bed hungry. It is an old joke innkeepers along the Kiso Road still tell on themselves — a piece of Edo-period popular literature folded into the landscape itself, so that travelers measure their own misfortunes against Yaji and Kita's.

Sankichi had laughed at that joke once, lightly. Then Matsumoto happened to him directly. A well-known painter had written him a letter of introduction to an inn with, the note promised, a lovely daughter and generous hospitality. No daughter appeared.

A maid seated him beside a nearly empty hibachi, and when he asked for noodles, the inn produced one crowded bowl where two had been requested, swimming in broth too thin to taste. Only later did he learn the painter who wrote that letter had once stayed five months at the inn without paying — and the innkeeper, assuming history would repeat, had settled the account in advance, on Sankichi.

## Isaku, the Cook Who Would Not Say Her Name

![Whispered Questions](/images/posts/the-lady-of-the-bellflower-pool-a-kiso-road-ghost-story-of-an-eyebrowless-bride/shot_034.jpeg)
*Whispered Questions.*


Narai offered the opposite. A modest lodging house beneath a thatched eave, palanquins hanging from its rafters beside strips of drying vegetable, gave Sankichi a ten-mat room with thick bear pelts, a hibachi heaped with coals, and a meal no humble inn should have produced — grilled yellowtail, a smoking omelet, kudzu-thickened broth, and five whole thrushes roasted head to tail. The inn's cook, a thin, watchful man named **Isaku**, came to apologize for the plainness of a meal that was anything but plain.

Warmed by sake after two cold days, Sankichi told Isaku a story from a Tokyo banquet — a geisha who had gone thrush-hunting in these very valleys and, rising from the hunting fire at dawn, her lips darkened from the birds she had eaten half-raw, looked for one instant less like a woman than like something the mountain itself had shaped. It was an idle anecdote, the kind travelers trade to fill an evening. Isaku's face went the color of cold ash.

He asked, with unusual precision, which slope, which valley above Niekawa, which river-district the geisha had come from — Yanagibashi, Shinbashi, elsewhere. He murmured that in deep mountain valleys there were things beyond ordinary human wisdom, then called toward the kitchen about the slowness of the lamps and said nothing further that night. The reader understands, before Sankichi does, that a door has been opened that cannot easily be shut again.

## The Woman in the Standing Mirror

![Room Tilts Outward](/images/posts/the-lady-of-the-bellflower-pool-a-kiso-road-ghost-story-of-an-eyebrowless-bride/shot_045.jpeg)
*Room Tilts Outward.*


The following evening, moved to a lower room beside the garden pond, Sankichi pressed his cheek to a bathhouse door and heard, unmistakably, a woman moving in water a maid swore no guest had entered. Returning to his room, he saw in the standing mirror a woman of perhaps twenty-six in indigo-grey silk, applying white powder before her own unseen reflection.

Her eyebrows had been shaved to nothing beneath the powder — the old custom called *hikimayu*, once worn by married women and by those in mourning, a visible mark that a woman belonged, in life or in grief, to someone no longer beside her. Her teeth, when she smiled, were blackened in the manner of *ohaguro*, the dye applied across the old provinces to signal that girlhood had closed behind a bride like a door.

These were not invented horrors. Both customs are documented features of premodern Japanese adornment, carried out of the Edo period and into the early Meiji decades before Westernizing reforms discouraged them. Their survival on a ghost, decades after they had fallen from fashion, reads here as a mark of something arrested in time rather than living in it.

She asked Sankichi, gently, whether it suited her. Then the room tilted, and he felt himself borne through the window on wings that were, impossibly, his own, falling into the black water of the garden pond — and woke in his own body, beside a tray the maid had only just set down. The horror here is not the mirror, nor the wings; it is the quiet, courteous repetition of a question asked, perhaps, to many men before him.

## The Bellflower Pool and the Reckoning at Narai

It was then that Isaku finally spoke, not as a cook addressing a guest but as a man setting down a weight he had carried too long alone. He led Sankichi, by lantern, past the kitchen stores to the small pond behind the inn — barely wider than a room, fringed that season with the withered stems of *kikyō*, the bellflower that gives the pool its name, **Kikyō-ga-ike**.

Isaku's account, as Sankichi later gave it, named no year precisely, only "before the railway came." A bride from a river-district house, promised to a Narai dyer's son, had shaved her brows and blackened her teeth in the old manner, completing the rites of marriage before the wedding day itself arrived. The groom's family, learning the bride's house had fallen into debt, withdrew the match without ceremony and without apology, leaving her already altered in body for a husband who would never claim her.

She was found at the pool's edge three days later, her borrowed wedding silk soaked through, her face composed as though she had only paused to check her reflection one last time. The village did not call it murder, and the tale does not ask the reader to either. It asks only that the reader notice how thoroughly a woman can be destroyed by customs meant to honor her — shaved and blackened for a marriage that existed, in the end, only on her own body.

Isaku was, by his own admission, a distant relation of the dyer's house, kept on at the inn generations later more as penance than employment. He confessed that guests who answered her question with admiration were rarely troubled twice; it was refusal, or worse, a flattery too easily withdrawn, that seemed to draw her favor toward the pond. Sankichi, the story notes with dry relief, had been asleep, and so had given no answer at all — perhaps the only honest response the dead woman had received in decades.

By morning the frost had taken the last of the bellflower stems, and Isaku would say nothing more on the matter, returning to the kitchen as though the lantern walk had not happened. Sankichi left Narai before noon, the thrush supper and the standing mirror both already taking on the soft unreality of something remembered rather than lived. The pond, local account insists even now, keeps its bellflowers paler than those grown elsewhere in the valley.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

The tale's survival owes everything to its print lineage rather than to fireside transmission. Unlike the orally gathered accounts **Yanagita Kunio** later compiled from Iwate farmers in *Tōno Monogatari* (1910), the Bellflower Pool story reaches us as finished literary prose, attributed within the **Aozora Bunko** archive to Izumi Kyōka's travel-writing output — a genre Kyōka used throughout his career to anchor supernatural unease in verifiable roadside geography, much as he did in *Kōya Hijiri* with the mountain paths of the Kii Peninsula.

That technique places the story in close company with **Ueda Akinari's** *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776), where drowned or betrayed women likewise return through water to pose a question the living cannot safely answer. The eyebrowless, blackened-toothed revenant also echoes the featureless-faced woman of **Lafcadio Hearn's** "Mujina," collected in *Kwaidan* (1904): both figures use the absence of an expected facial feature to transform a familiar domestic encounter into something the mind refuses to process cleanly.

Narai-juku itself survives as one of Japan's designated Important Preservation Districts for Groups of Traditional Buildings, its Edo-period eaves and *kiso-hinoki* timber maintained largely as the story describes them. No official record documents a drowning matching Isaku's account, and no shrine marks a grave — a silence the story itself anticipates, since the dyer's house, by Isaku's telling, had every reason to let the matter be forgotten.

What remains, beyond the inn and the pond, is the custom-history the tale quietly preserves: *hikimayu* and *ohaguro*, genuine markers of Edo-to-Meiji womanhood, repurposed here as the visible residue of a promise broken before it was ever kept. The story's lasting unease is not the bend of the ceiling or the pull of black water, but the ordinariness of the question at its center — *does it suit me* — asked by a woman who was made to dress for a wedding that history simply declined to hold.
