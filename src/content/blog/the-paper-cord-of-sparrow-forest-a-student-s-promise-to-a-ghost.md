The oil lamp burns low in a Sendai boarding house, and somewhere beneath the floorboards, a footstep begins its climb. It does not announce itself. It simply arrives — too light, too uncertain, as though the thing producing it were still deciding whether it had the right to exist. This is where the record known as *The Paper Cord of Sparrow Forest: A Student's Promise to a Ghost* begins, and where any honest retelling must begin as well: not with explanation, but with the sound of a stair creaking under weight that may not have been there at all.

## The Footstep on the Stairs

![Jinnaka's Gentle Request](/images/posts/the-paper-cord-of-sparrow-forest-a-student-s-promise-to-a-ghost/shot_012.jpeg)
*Jinnaka's Gentle Request.*


The account survives in a regional compilation of **Tōhoku** ghost testimony, the kind of *kaidan* — supernatural chronicle — that local writers in the late nineteenth century assembled from witness statement rather than literary invention. Its final line notes that the Sendai police retained an official file on the resulting death, filed as unresolved. That detail alone separates this story from pure folklore and plants it somewhere closer to regional journalism with an open wound.

The student at its center was the son of a prosperous farming family near **Gifu**, sitting for term examinations at a higher school four hundred kilometers from home. On a close June night, the figure in his doorway wore a white summer robe far too early for the season — a detail he noted, almost idly, before he recognized the face beneath it.

It was **Jinnaka**, a childhood friend now working as a prefectural clerk, pale and soft-spoken as the student remembered him. He should not have been in Sendai at all. He offered no explanation, only a request: meet him the next midnight at the stone lantern in **Suzume ga Mori**, Sparrow Forest, beside the old shrine. "A small thing," he said. "I wouldn't ask anything wrong of you." Trust, more than curiosity, carried the promise forward.

## A Promise Tied in Paper

![Tying the Paper Cord](/images/posts/the-paper-cord-of-sparrow-forest-a-student-s-promise-to-a-ghost/shot_022.jpeg)
*Tying the Paper Cord.*


The following night arrived heavy with the green smell of early summer leaves, the kind of air that presses down before a rain that never comes. Jinnaka was waiting at the lantern, already lit by a flame with no clear source. Between his fingers he held a *koyori* — a thin cord twisted from paper, long used across rural **Japanese folklore** in charm-work and binding rites meant to seal a vow or hold a spirit to a place.

"Tie this around my left forefinger," he said. "Three turns, and tie it tight." The student, assuming some rustic superstition carried from home, complied without much thought — winding the pale, oddly translucent finger three times, knotting the paper closed. Jinnaka thanked him, asked him to go home, and promised to return the favor properly the next day. He did not explain why the ritual mattered. He never would.

The student walked back through the dark irritated rather than afraid, certain only that his old friend had grown strange in the years apart. It is worth pausing on this ordinariness. Nothing in the exchange reads as menacing in the moment — no rattling chains, no cold wind, only a quiet favor between friends. The dread, as in so much of **Edo period** and Meiji-era ghost literature, arrives only in retrospect, once the listener understands what the ritual actually was.

## The Body by the Stone Lantern

![Unseen Evidence](/images/posts/the-paper-cord-of-sparrow-forest-a-student-s-promise-to-a-ghost/shot_032.jpeg)
*Unseen Evidence.*


The student woke unwell the next morning, his shoulder aching along the same line where the paper cord had pressed the night before. At the communal well, the neighborhood women were not drawing water but whispering it: a man had been found dead in Sparrow Forest, dressed in Western clothing, beside the very lantern, with no wound anywhere on his body.

Jinnaka had worn a white robe, not a suit — a small relief that lasted only until the student forced himself to the scene. Among a loose ring of twenty onlookers, an elderly notable lifted the corner of the straw mat covering the dead man's face. It was a stranger, heavier, past forty. The relief did not survive a second look. Wound three times around the dead man's outstretched forefinger, knotted exactly as the student had knotted it, was an identical paper cord.

No newspaper of the time explained the death, and none mentioned the cord at all — as though no reporter had thought it worth recording, or as though none had thought to look. For two days the student waited in Sendai for Jinnaka to come and collect his promised thanks. Jinnaka never came. Unwell and unsettled, the student left for Gifu before the week was finished.

## Gifu, and the Debt That Could Not Be Repaid

![Tragedy in the Weaving Room](/images/posts/the-paper-cord-of-sparrow-forest-a-student-s-promise-to-a-ghost/shot_042.jpeg)
*Tragedy in the Weaving Room.*


At his family's house, the record turns. Days before that midnight meeting in Sendai, Jinnaka had already lost his clerkship — dismissed, the account specifies, for refusing to let his section chief take his younger sister as a wife. The chief had a known history of cruelty toward women, having divorced three or four wives already. Jinnaka's refusal cost him his livelihood, and brother and sister lived afterward on almost nothing, she weaving on a rented loom while he pressed every contact he had for new work.

At a bank in Gifu, seeking employment, Jinnaka found himself met with unexplained contempt — until a friend showed him a regional newspaper. Its editor, privately allied with the vindictive section chief, had published an accusation against brother and sister of the ugliest and least true kind. Jinnaka walked home through streets he could not later describe and found his sister already dead by her own hand in the weaving room. He laid her out with the dignity custom demanded, and in that same room, beside her, ended his own life.

By every account from the Gifu household, Jinnaka had already been dead for some time when a white-robed visitor climbed a Sendai staircase to ask a quiet favor of an old friend. The man discovered at the lantern the next dawn — bound by a steady hand that did not tremble — was later confirmed to be the very editor who had written the article that killed them both. He had traveled to Sendai that week, telling his innkeeper only that he had somewhere to go.

## Threads Across Four Hundred Kilometers

No mechanism was ever offered for how a dead man's request could cross four hundred kilometers of railway and mountain dark to bind a stranger's fingers around a fate he knew nothing of. The paper cord itself is the detail that gives the tale its particular folkloric weight — not a curse spoken aloud, but a vow sealed in an object, delegated to an unwitting hand. This structure echoes older Japanese ghost literature, where vengeance is rarely direct; it travels through objects, proxies, and unknowing accomplices.

Readers familiar with **Ueda Akinari**'s *Ugetsu Monogatari* will recognize the shape of a promise that outlives the one who made it, carried across distance by a figure who seems ordinary until the moment of recognition arrives too late. This is offered as historical interpretation, not direct lineage — no record ties this Sendai account to Akinari's eighteenth-century text. The resemblance belongs to a broader current in Japanese supernatural storytelling, not to a traceable borrowing.

What distinguishes *The Paper Cord of Sparrow Forest* within this current is its evidentiary posture. It does not ask to be believed as myth. It is framed, deliberately, as testimony — a regional *kaidan* built from witness statement, anchored by an open police file, a named town, a dated newspaper article. Whether or not one credits the supernatural mechanism, the human tragedy beneath it — a sister's death, a brother's grief, an editor's unanswered cruelty — requires no ghost to be devastating.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

Regional Tōhoku *kaidan* collections of the Meiji period occupy an unusual space between folklore and local journalism. Unlike the earlier Edo-period compilations of **Asai Ryōi**'s *Otogi Bōko*, which drew heavily on Chinese ghost literature reshaped for Japanese audiences, these later Tōhoku accounts were often gathered directly from townspeople, filed alongside newspaper clippings and, in rarer cases, police records — precisely the kind of archival residue this story claims for itself.

**Lafcadio Hearn**, writing in the same decades from Matsue and later Tokyo, documented a comparable style of provincial testimony in his own essays on Japanese ghosts, though no surviving collection of his work names this particular Sendai case. The comparison is offered as context for the genre, not as a claim of direct record. Similarly, **Yanagita Kunio**'s later *Tōno Monogatari* (1910) established the model of regional oral testimony preserved near-verbatim — the same editorial instinct that likely shaped whatever Sendai compiler first set this account to paper.

What remains today is modest. Suzume ga Mori still stands at the edge of Sendai in local memory, and the stone lantern, by every account passed down, is still lit at dusk. The newspaper that named an unidentified man dead without wound in 1888 has never been definitively traced to a surviving archive, and the detail of the paper cord — the single physical proof binding two deaths across four hundred kilometers — was never printed at all. It survives only because someone, decades later, thought a dead man's steady hand was worth remembering.
