The lantern at the Sugamo temple fence turned on windless nights, and no one who watched it could say why. This detail opens the account we now call **The Lantern of Nishimori**, and it is worth pausing on before the road to Ishigaki even begins. A paper globe spinning on its wire axis, oak leaves dead still above it — this is not the language of a jump-scare. It is the language of the *kaidan* tradition at its most patient: a small wrongness, witnessed, then carried forward into a larger one.

## The Turning Lantern and the Logic of Dread

![Four Students in a Bar](/images/posts/the-lantern-of-nishimori-a-dead-friend-returns-on-a-moonless-road/shot_010.jpeg)
*Four Students in a Bar.*


Japanese ghost literature rarely opens with violence. It opens with an anomaly too small to report to anyone but a drinking companion. **Yamamoto**, the young lodger who first noticed the Sugamo lantern, does not investigate it, does not confront it, does not even speak of it until pressed. He simply moves his lodgings and keeps the memory folded away.

This pattern — the witness who flees rather than explains — recurs throughout Edo-period kaidan collections. It is the behavior of a culture that treated the uncanny not as a puzzle to be solved but as a boundary not to be tested twice. The lantern's slow, complete rotation, with no wind to account for it, functions as a kind of folkloric warning shot: the natural order has been nudged, however slightly, and something is now listening.

That the story Yamamoto eventually tells becomes the catalyst for a far stranger night is itself a structural signature of the genre. A minor haunting, confessed over beer, opens a door that a larger haunting walks through. The *kaidan* rarely isolates its hauntings; they gather, like weather.

## Four Students on the Road to Ishigaki

![Rippling Water and Insect Sounds](/images/posts/the-lantern-of-nishimori-a-dead-friend-returns-on-a-moonless-road/shot_019.jpeg)
*Rippling Water and Insect Sounds.*


The bar near **Hongō Sanchōme**, the restless suggestion to go "somewhere, anywhere," the last bottle of beer drunk standing at the station — this is recognizably the texture of early twentieth-century student life in Tokyo, when trams still rang their bells down Hongō and university men wore the square black cap that marked them as *gakusei*. The narrator, **Hirayama**, is careful to admit that he and his companions were simply the ones who had stayed behind in August: too poor, too idle, or too fond of drink to leave for the coast like everyone else.

Their destination, the inn at **Ishigaki**, lay some three *ri* — nearly twelve kilometers — of unlit farmland and pine groves from the station. They chose to walk it near midnight, in a warm sea wind under a moonless, starless sky. The practical detail matters here: this was not a road built for ghost stories. It was a road four young men had likely walked before, sober, in daylight, without incident.

The account lingers on sensory texture rather than event for nearly its entire middle stretch. Mosquitoes loud enough to forbid rest. A footbridge. Pine forest on one side, black paddies on the other. The surf mixing with frog-song until conversation stopped on its own, for reasons none of the four examined too closely. Hirayama's later remark — that a fifth set of footsteps seemed to keep pace with them, belonging to no one visible — is offered almost as an aside, the way a true account offers its strangest details: without emphasis, because the teller does not yet know which detail will matter.

## The Red Light on the Dunes

![Recognized Face](/images/posts/the-lantern-of-nishimori-a-dead-friend-returns-on-a-moonless-road/shot_027.jpeg)
*Recognized Face.*


When the small red light first appeared ahead of them, swaying low to the ground, the four reacted not with fear but with relief. A paper lantern approaching on a dark road was, in the folk logic of the Japanese countryside, the oldest reassurance available: another traveler, awake, ordinary, carrying fire against the same dark you carried yours against. This inversion — the *comfort* of an approaching light, later curdling into its opposite — is the hinge on which the entire tale turns.

The figure behind the lantern wore a student's uniform and the same square cap as the four men themselves. When Hirayama raised the light to the face, he recognized an old schoolmate: **Nishimori**, a young man he had known at higher school before their paths drifted apart, as paths between classmates often do once university divides them into separate faculties.

The account pauses here to supply Nishimori's family history, and this, too, follows a recognizable shape in Japanese ghost narrative — the restless dead are rarely strangers to grievance. Nishimori's father, eldest son of a once-considerable rural estate, had been quietly dispossessed by a younger uncle grown wealthy in the Yokohama iron trade. The grandfather's death left the uncle in possession of property that should, by custom, have passed to Nishimori's father. *Historical interpretation:* the humiliation of disinheritance within an Edo-descended family structure, where primogeniture carried not only wealth but social legitimacy, was understood at the time as a wound capable of killing by degrees — which, the narrator reports plainly, it did.

## The Gift That Should Not Have Been Given

![Hirayama walking, the borrowed lantern swinging gently in his hand, casting an oscillating pool of light on the damp](/images/posts/the-lantern-of-nishimori-a-dead-friend-returns-on-a-moonless-road/shot_036.jpeg)
*Hirayama walking, the borrowed lantern swinging gently in his hand, casting an oscillating pool of light on the damp.*


Nishimori, pleasant and unhurried, asked where his old friends were headed at such an hour. Told they meant to walk on to Ishigaki, he smiled — Hirayama recalled the smile with unusual clarity, years later, in the telling — and offered his own lantern for the remainder of their journey. *"Leave it anywhere along the way,"* he said. *"I expect you're headed for the Ishigaki. Leave it there. I'll collect it myself."*

He turned and walked back into the dark, his footsteps swallowed almost immediately by the sound of the surf. Hirayama walked on perhaps five or six paces with the borrowed light swinging in his hand before one of the others — no one could later agree which — spoke in a voice gone suddenly cold: *"Wait. That's strange. Didn't he die last month?"*

The silence that followed is where the story does its quietest, most effective work. No thunderclap, no apparition dissolving into mist — only the frogs continuing their indifferent chorus, the surf continuing its indifferent motion, and Hirayama's own memory surfacing on its own, unbidden: a rumor, half-forgotten, that Nishimori had lost his mind and taken his own life weeks before. All four men cried out together, without agreeing to, and ran.

This restraint is deliberate, and it is worth naming as a technique rather than an accident. The tragedy — a young man's despair, his death, his apparent return — is never staged for the reader. It arrives as a *sealed fact*, delivered secondhand in a single sentence, the way the worst news in life usually does: not dramatized, simply remembered at the worst possible moment.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

What makes **The Lantern of Nishimori** unusual among Japanese ghost accounts is its final, almost anticlimactic detail. The manuscript does not record how far the four men ran, what they told the innkeeper at Ishigaki, or whether they spoke of the encounter again that night. It records only this: the lantern itself, the one physical object capable of confirming the story, was lost somewhere in the panicked run between the dunes and the inn door, and none of the four ever went back to look for it.

The student who eventually told this account to the keeper of the record — in the secondhand, once-removed manner typical of orally preserved *kaidan*, where the teller is rarely the sole witness — admitted, many years later, to only one regret. Not fear of the ghost. Not grief for his friend. Regret at letting go of the lantern, the single piece of evidence that might have proven, one way or another, that any of it had truly happened.

This closing admission places the tale firmly within a lineage stretching back through **Ueda Akinari's** *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776) and **Asai Ryōi's** *Otogi Bōko* (1666), both of which favor the encounter with the returning dead over any demand for proof. Like the doomed scholar Hasebe in Akinari's *Kibitsu no Kama*, or the wife who returns past her own death in *Asajigatsuyu*, Nishimori is granted no monstrous transformation — only an unnervingly ordinary politeness, which is precisely what makes his appearance so difficult to dismiss.

Lafcadio Hearn, writing a generation later in *Kwaidan* (1904), observed that the Japanese ghost rarely announces itself as a ghost; it simply behaves as the living once did, until a single detail — a missing shadow, a borrowed object, a fact remembered too late — reveals the gap. The Nishimori account belongs to that same quiet tradition: a *chōchin* changing hands on a dark road, a smile, a direction given, and then absence.

No site marks the Ishigaki road today with any certainty, and the inn itself, if it still stands, is not identified by name in any surviving archive known to this publication. What remains is the account itself — unverifiable, secondhand, missing its one material proof — which is, perhaps, the most faithful ending a story like this could have.

- Asai Ryōi (1666), *Otogi Bōko* — early Edo-period kaidan anthology establishing the returning-dead encounter as literary form.
