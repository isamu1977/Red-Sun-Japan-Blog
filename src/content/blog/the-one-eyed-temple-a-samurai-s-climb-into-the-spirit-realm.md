The andon's flame had guttered to a thread of orange when the retainer understood, with the cold clarity of a man returning to his senses mid-fall, that he was no longer alone in the room. A seated shape waited at the edge of the light. He had met that face only hours before, bent over a go board in a temple that, by every law of the mountain he knew, had no right to exist.

This is the shape the story takes in the surviving account: a samurai of the **Kishū** branch of the Tokugawa house, a hot-spring inn at **Yumoto onsen**, and a mountain above the Hakone ravine that the innkeeper called, without drama, a place where "the honorable ones" hold their revels after dark. What follows is not a retelling of the video's narration but a walk through the terrain the story claims for itself — the ravine, the thicket, the one-eyed temple, and the long archival shadow the tale has cast since.

## The Mountain Above Yumoto Onsen

![Samurai's Confident Laugh](/images/posts/the-one-eyed-temple-a-samurai-s-climb-into-the-spirit-realm/shot_013.jpeg)
*Samurai's Confident Laugh.*


Hakone sat astride the **Tōkaidō**, the great coastal road linking Edo to the western domains, and its hot-spring inns were a customary stopping point for daimyō processions and lone retainers alike. The account places its samurai there on ordinary business, idling over a board game with his innkeeper on an unremarkable afternoon. It is precisely this ordinariness — a bored traveler, a pleasant view, an idle suggestion — that gives the opening its unease. *Kaidan* rarely announce themselves. They begin as small decisions.

The story is preserved, as the record states, among the oral and manuscript traditions of the Hakone hot-spring district, within the broader category the Japanese call *tengu-kakushi* — "spiriting away by tengu." These narratives describe travelers, woodcutters, and children who vanish into mountain terrain and return altered, sickened, or not at all. Regional folklorists later gathered such accounts, and modern *yōkai* survey work, including archival cross-referencing at institutions such as **Nichibunken** (the International Research Center for Japanese Studies), has helped preserve this specific strand of Hakone lore within the wider *tengu-kakushi* catalogue.

The innkeeper's warning deserves attention on its own terms. He does not forbid the mountain outright. He draws a legal distinction a feudal audience would have recognized instantly: entering in ignorance carries no penalty, but entering *with knowledge* of what waits there invites "a reckoning." It is a warning about intention, not geography — the mountain punishes defiance, not trespass. The samurai's laughter at tengu and *kodama* is therefore not mere bravado; it is a formal rejection of the warning's terms, spoken aloud, and in the folkloric logic of the tale, binding.

## The Climb Through the Thicket

![Composite Blossoms and Green Felt](/images/posts/the-one-eyed-temple-a-samurai-s-climb-into-the-spirit-realm/shot_024.jpeg)
*Composite Blossoms and Green Felt.*


The ravine crossing — bamboo wicket gate, plank bridge, opposite slope — reads like a boundary ritual, the kind of threshold crossing common to Japanese ghost and spirit narratives, where a bridge or gate marks the last point of safe return. The samurai crosses alone, exactly as he vowed, and the mountain responds immediately. A gray-striped serpent nearly six feet long rears at eye level before dropping away; moments later, underfoot, a second snake shows a stump where its tail should be, severed by some unseen cause the account never explains.

Snakes recur throughout Japanese folklore as boundary creatures — neither fully of the human world nor fully apart from it — and their appearance here functions less as a physical threat than as an escalating signal: the terrain itself is now actively aware of him. The thicket thickens until he must force the undergrowth apart with both hands, and it closes behind him "without a sound, as though the mountain were healing itself of his passage." Below, in the ravine, a repeating sound — *dou, dou, do, do, dou* — rolls upward, which the account allows might be the river, though it withholds certainty.

What the record does state plainly is the paradox of his nerve: the deeper the thicket grows, the steadier he becomes. This inversion — courage rising precisely where dread should — is a recognizable feature of tales in which a human mind has already, without knowing it, crossed into territory governed by rules other than its own. The unseasonal field of blossoms that follows, flowers of a dozen incompatible seasons blooming together under impossible birdsong, confirms it. Distance itself behaves wrongly; a clearing that should measure a few hundred paces stretches to the length of eight city blocks.

## Sekiganzan Ichimokuji: The Temple of the Single Eye

![Samurai Addresses Monk](/images/posts/the-one-eyed-temple-a-samurai-s-climb-into-the-spirit-realm/shot_035.jpeg)
*Samurai Addresses Monk.*


The gate appears abruptly, and relief follows just as fast — a temple, however strange, still reads to a feudal mind as consecrated ground, a place where danger should not follow. The account dismantles that assumption methodically. The gilt Buddha on the altar is missing its left eye. The old monk at the go board is missing his left eye. The young acolyte beside him is missing his right.

The *rakan* figures along the wall are each missing an eye. The painted heavenly maidens on the ceiling, the cranes and pheasants on the right wall, the lions and *kirin* on the left — every single face in that room, carved or painted, shares the same mutilation, distributed left and right with what the samurai himself calls, in half-amused disbelief, a thorough matching.

The old monk gives the place its name without further explanation: **Sekiganzan Ichimokuji**, the Temple of the Single Eye, on the Mountain of the Lone Pupil. He adds only that this is no place for men, and asks, with what the account describes as a trembling, cold sorrow, how the samurai found his way there at all. It is not hostility in his voice. It is closer to grief — the tone of someone who has watched this exact scene recur before and already knows how it ends.

Pride, the account makes explicit, will not permit the samurai to withdraw now. He makes an offering of gold before the one-eyed Buddha and voices a wish that the record renders as dry, almost reflexive mockery — that the gift might open the statue's remaining eye. *Historical interpretation*: in the moral grammar of Edo-period *kaidan*, an offering made in mockery rather than reverence rarely goes unanswered kindly, and this tale observes that convention precisely. The statue's mouth opens. It laughs. And the laughter spreads through every painted and carved face in the hall at once — not fury, the account is careful to note, but the particular laughter reserved for something too foolish to be worth anger.

## The Flight Back to Edo

![Abrupt Stop and Fall](/images/posts/the-one-eyed-temple-a-samurai-s-climb-into-the-spirit-realm/shot_046.jpeg)
*Abrupt Stop and Fall.*


Terror does what pride could not and sends him running. Outside the gate, dusk has thickened unnaturally fast, and two palanquin bearers wait at the roadside as though summoned in advance. He notices, and says nothing, that each bearer is missing his left eye. Pride holds its grip even in flight. The litter departs the ground with a velocity the account insists no human legs could produce, wind tearing past "like a gale," the bearers instructing him twice to keep his eyes shut until told otherwise.

When the motion stops and he is told to step down, he finds himself not on any mountain path but before the walled Edo townhouse of the Kishū house itself — a hundred miles, by any ordinary reckoning, from the ravine he had climbed perhaps an hour earlier. A passerby's lantern confirms the address without being asked twice. The samurai's fear here is doubled and specific: fear of what has been done to him, and fear, sharper still, of what his own house would make of a retainer bested by tengu and tree-spirits. He gives his name at the gate, takes his quarters, and says nothing to anyone.

## The Monk's Final Visit

The old monk does not remain on the mountain. He arrives that same night at the samurai's bedside, in the dim glow of the night lamp, and speaks a single line the account preserves exactly: that however proudly a man stands, a human life is no more than dew on the grass, and no one knows when it ends. The samurai draws his sword. The shape dissolves. He searches the room and finds nothing, sheathes the blade, and sleeps — only to wake to the same seated shape, the same sorrowful voice, the same sentence.

This repeats across the following nights, and then, the account states without embellishment, the monk begins appearing by daylight as well, visible now to the visitors who come to sit with the increasingly gaunt samurai. He wastes through the following weeks in the precise manner of the monk's own wasted frame, until he dies. The record closes on a line offered flatly, without further comment: that anyone who afterward lodged in that same barracks room always found something strange waiting there.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

The tale belongs to a recognizable Edo-period genre in which the forbidden mountain and the uncanny inn converge — a lineage that runs, in spirit if not in direct textual descent, from **Asai Ryōi's** *Otogi Bōko* (1666), with its compiled accounts of hauntings reaching into ordinary lodging houses, through to **Ueda Akinari's** *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776), where travelers who cross a threshold out of pride or curiosity find the spirit world keeping its own account of the transaction. The one-eyed motif itself echoes a broader iconography cataloged later by **Toriyama Sekien** in his *yōkai* encyclopedias, where single-eyed and incomplete figures recur as mountain apparitions distinct from the tengu proper.

What the Hakone account preserves that these literary works sometimes smooth over is its procedural quality — the innkeeper's precise legal warning, the bearers' instructions about closed eyes, the monk's unhurried nightly return. **Lafcadio Hearn**, writing of Japanese ghost tradition a century later, observed that the most unsettling *kaidan* are rarely those of violence but those of quiet, patient insistence — a presence that simply will not stop returning. This tale fits that register closely. Nothing in it is explained. The mountain is never named a tengu's domain outright; it is simply shown to behave like one.

Modern Hakone retains the hot-spring geography the account describes, and Yumoto onsen remains a working inn district along what was once the Tōkaidō, though no specific barracks room or temple site has been publicly identified with the tale. The philosophical core the monk leaves behind — dew on grass, no one knowing when it ends — is itself a recognizable Buddhist formulation of impermanence, and its presence here, spoken by a figure whose own face bears the story's central wound, suggests the tale was never primarily about tengu at all, but about the cost of refusing to recognize a warning once it has been given.

### Primary Texts & Scholarly References

- Asai Ryōi (1666), *Otogi Bōko* — early compiled Japanese ghost-story anthology establishing the haunted-lodging narrative pattern.
- Ueda Akinari (1776), *Ugetsu Monogatari* — foundational collection of tales concerning travelers who cross into spirit-governed terrain.
- Toriyama Sekien (18th century), *Gazu Hyakki Yagyō* and related *yōkai* encyclopedias — iconographic catalog of mountain spirits and single-eyed apparitions.
