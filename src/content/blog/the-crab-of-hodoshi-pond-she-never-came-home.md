The water at Hodoshi-ike has never run dry, not once, in all the summers the old farmers of Takaoka district can remember. That single fact — a pond fed by no visible spring, holding its clear green stillness through drought after drought — is the first and last mystery of this story. Everything else that happened near its banks, the local kaidan tradition insists, followed from that unnatural permanence.

## The Stone Basin Beneath Hiura Pass

![Frozen in Thought](/images/posts/the-crab-of-hodoshi-pond-she-never-came-home/shot_012.jpeg)
*Frozen in Thought.*


The setting belongs to a specific, recoverable geography: the Takaoka district of old Tosa province, in the years when the land below Hiura Pass fell under the Sakawa domain. This was terraced country, chestnut groves and rice paddies strung between the villages of Nagano and Tonami, with the pass itself cresting at a hollow where the Hodoshi-ike sat half-shadowed by mixed timber. Below it ran a stream where the women of the villages washed their clothes each morning, kneeling at a stone basin the current had worn smooth in the rock.

This is not a tale lifted from a famous anthology or dressed up by a city publisher. It survives the way most *kaidan* of rural Japan survive — as spoken memory among farming households, later set down in a regional folklore record with names retained and no explanation offered. That refusal to explain is itself characteristic of the genre. The Edo-period chroniclers who gathered such accounts rarely interpreted them; they simply preserved the sequence of events and trusted the reader to feel the weight of it.

## A Village Girl, a Stranger on the Path

![Extreme close-up on a fleeting, sharp flash of dark violet light](/images/posts/the-crab-of-hodoshi-pond-she-never-came-home/shot_023.jpeg)
*Extreme close-up on a fleeting, sharp flash of dark violet light.*


Otane was washing clothes the morning it began, sleeves tied back with a red cord, a white cloth over her hair in the manner of young working women. She had finished two garments when a voice called her name from the path above — and turned to find a boy of perhaps fifteen, dressed in a purple *furisode*, the long-sleeved robe associated with a temple page. She assumed he served at Shakuzenji, whose Yakushi hall she sometimes visited, since his path led toward it.

"Otane. Have you forgotten me?" he asked, and smiled in a way she could not read. She had never seen him before. When she said so, he answered only that she would understand soon enough, and that they would meet again before long. He walked up toward the pass and was gone into the trees before she thought to call after him. She stood a long while after, the washing forgotten in her hands.

That evening she could not eat. At the shared bathhouse, heated that night by a neighbor named Yohei, an old man teased her about someone waiting for her somewhere, and a woman leaving the bath laughed in agreement. Otane said nothing. It is worth noting, in the account's own careful phrasing, that she felt relief the voice had not belonged to Inosaku — and then, strangely, disappointment that it had not belonged to Denzō either. The story asks us to hold both feelings at once, and does not resolve the tension between them.

## The Orchard, and the Shape That Intervened

![Rotten Leaf Litter](/images/posts/the-crab-of-hodoshi-pond-she-never-came-home/shot_033.jpeg)
*Rotten Leaf Litter.*


Walking home through the mikan and loquat orchard below the house, Otane was stopped by Inosaku, a young man who had long wished to speak with her — a fact well known in the village. What followed that night went beyond speaking. When she tried to pull away, his grip closed around her and would not release.

It was then, the record says, that something flashed before his eyes — violet-dark, shaped like a blade, or a claw. Inosaku threw his hands to his face and fled through the mist faster than his own body seemed to allow, crying out as he went. Otane, freed so suddenly she nearly fell, saw only his back vanish at the gate and assumed some passerby had frightened him off.

No one had come. She never learned what had actually driven him away, and the account offers no further comment — only the bare fact that something had intervened, and that it had chosen that moment to do so.

From the following day, Otane was no longer herself. Sent to wash two or three garments, her mother would find her still standing at the water's edge at midday, motionless. When Denzō — a Tonami day laborer, a capable sumo wrestler known for a gentleness far beyond his build — came by that evening as he often did, she barely answered him. It had never been like this before; she had always urged him to stay later. He left with a look her mother did not like.

## The Comb Among the Reeds

![Denzō's Tense Face](/images/posts/the-crab-of-hodoshi-pond-she-never-came-home/shot_044.jpeg)
*Denzō's Tense Face.*


The next morning Otane dressed with unusual care, a trace of make-up applied as though for someone's eyes, and walked out toward the stream as if drawn forward on a thread she could not see. Her mother checked on her half an hour later and found the washing soaking at the usual place, and no Otane anywhere.

The household searched the fields through the day — the grandfather called down from the barley, the father summoned from the rice paddies, three search parties sent out by afternoon. It was the party crossing Hiura Pass, almost as an afterthought, that thought to check the pond. Among the reeds at Hodoshi-ike's edge, someone found a boxwood comb, freshly fallen, still beaded with water. Inosaku, present among the searchers, said at once it was Otane's — he knew, he said, because she had been going there. No one thought to ask him how he knew that.

What happened next is the hinge of the entire account. Inosaku volunteered to dive in himself, stripped to the waist, and went under after a single breath. The men waited the length of a pipe of tobacco.

Then, some two arm-spans from where he had vanished, the surface bloomed suddenly into a dark stillness, and a shape rose belly-up. It was Inosaku — his right arm gone, taken clean, the wound still fresh.

The men on the bank fled without a word among them, and no one ever went back into the Hodoshi-ike to search further. The family took Otane's disappearance as her death, named the day her memorial, and began their prayers — though no body of hers, unlike his, was ever recovered from that water.

## The Vision at Miko-ga-naro

Of everyone who grieved her, Denzō grieved longest and most quietly, often stopping at the house on his way home simply to sit with Otane's mother. Returning alone across Hiura Pass one night heavy with coming rain, he heard a voice call from no direction he could place — *kona, kona*, come here, come here. He refused it, turning instead onto the narrow path toward Kokuzō Mountain, away from the pond.

That detour led him into a hollow called Miko-ga-naro, the clearing of the shrine maiden, where something began to show itself on a great flat rock. First a court lady in a *jūni-hitoe*, the twelve-layered robe of old aristocracy; then an armored warrior; then a gold-lit palace; then a statue of Fudō Myōō, flame rising from all four sides. Denzō, no coward, laughed scornfully at each transformation — until the statue dissolved into something far larger: a crab whose shell seemed to span twelve tatami mats laid end to end, and seated calmly upon its back, unmistakable, Otane.

Denzō understood in that instant what had taken Inosaku's arm in the pond. He went for the creature with his fists raised, and his body went numb before he reached it. He woke at dawn in the wet grass of Miko-ga-naro with no memory of lying down, and walked home in silence. For the rest of his life, the record notes, he never returned to the water atop Hiura Pass, and never once spoke of what he had seen that night to anyone who might have asked.

## Archival Roots: Tosa Kaidan and the Vengeful Water Spirit

This tale belongs to a broad current in Japanese folklore in which still water — a pond, a well, a bend in a river — becomes the threshold where a wronged woman's fate is settled outside the reach of her village. The pattern echoes through better-documented Edo-period collections. **Asai Ryōi**'s *Otogi Bōko* (1666) and **Ueda Akinari**'s *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776) both return repeatedly to women whose deaths at or near water leave the living permanently altered, haunted less by a visible ghost than by an unresolved moral debt.

What distinguishes the Hodoshi-ike account from those literary classics is its lack of polish. There is no poet-narrator here shaping the tale toward a moral, no refined courtly diction. It reads instead like the regional *kaidan* tradition that **Yanagita Kunio** later catalogued in works such as *Tōno Monogatari* (1910) — plain, local, insistent on real place names and real family names, offered as testimony rather than literature. The temple of Shakuzenji, the stream basin, the pass itself: these are not narrative conveniences but fixed coordinates, the kind that mark an oral account still tethered to a living community rather than reworked for an audience of readers.

### The Crab as a Figure in the Water Yōkai Tradition

Giant or transformed crabs appear only sparingly across the catalogued *yōkai* of artists like **Toriyama Sekien**, whose *Gazu Hyakki Yagyō* (1776) devoted far more attention to the *kappa* and other freshwater spirits that drown or abduct the unwary. The Hodoshi-ike crab sits closer to that general tradition of water-dwelling entities that punish trespass or claim the living through drowning, than to any single named monster in the standard bestiaries. Its size — famously rendered as wider than twelve tatami mats — functions less as taxonomy than as a measure of the horror's scale, a rhetorical device common to oral accounts that had no illustrated precedent to draw from.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

The account of Otane, Inosaku, and Denzō survives today chiefly within the local *kaidan* record of the Takaoka district, part of what is now Kōchi Prefecture — the modern successor to old Tosa province. Unlike the nationally circulated tales collected by **Lafcadio Hearn** in *Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things* (1904), this story never achieved wide literary adaptation; it remained, by design or accident, a village memory rather than a published classic. That obscurity is itself part of its value to the historical record: it shows what a *kaidan* looked like before any author's hand smoothed its edges.

The moral architecture, however, tracks precisely with the wider tradition. A violation goes unpunished by human law — Inosaku's assault in the orchard is never reported, never adjudicated — and the punishment instead arrives from the water itself, total and final. Otane's fate remains genuinely unresolved; no body answers for her beyond a single comb among the reeds. It is this absence, more than any monstrous reveal, that gives the Hodoshi-ike account its lasting unease: the pond keeps its answer, the way it has always kept its water, through every dry season on record.
