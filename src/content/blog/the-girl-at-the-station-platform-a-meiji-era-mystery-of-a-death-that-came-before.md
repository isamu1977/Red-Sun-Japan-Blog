The noon gate at Kōzu station opened onto sudden light, sharp after a morning of rain, and a voice found her in the press of bodies — not shouted, not urgent, almost gentle, spoken close against her ear. *Keiko-san wa shinimashita.* Keiko-san has died. She turned. No face nearby belonged to anyone she knew. This is the account at the center of **the girl at the station platform, a Meiji-era mystery of a death that came before** the telegram, before the letter, before any human mouth in Tokyo could have known to speak the words at all.

## Archival Roots: Okamoto Kidō and the Witness Who Would Not Be Named

![Water Droplets on Leaf](/images/posts/the-girl-at-the-station-platform-a-meiji-era-mystery-of-a-death-that-came-before/shot_014.jpeg)
*Water Droplets on Leaf.*


The story survives because one man made it his life's work to listen. **Okamoto Kidō** — playwright, journalist, and the most meticulous chronicler of *kaidan* working across the late Meiji and Taishō eras — collected first-person ghost testimonies with the same discipline a historian brings to a court record. He did not invent plots. He interviewed the people who claimed the events happened to them, weighed their sincerity, and set down what remained after the weighing.

This particular account he preserved in the words of an unnamed witness, recorded in his papers only as the wife of a Mr. M. She was, by her own telling, still a schoolgirl when it happened, in the years immediately following the Russo-Japanese War, which closed in the autumn of 1905. Kidō kept her route exact — Shimbashi, Kōzu, Odawara, Yugawara, the old Tōkaidō line stations named one by one, as though precision of geography might stand in for proof.

The chronicle survives today in printed anthologies of Kidō's collected ghost stories and in the digital holdings of the **Aozora Bunko** archive, catalogued among the testimonies he judged, after direct questioning, to be sincerely told. It belongs to a broader current of Japanese folklore in which the dead — or something standing in for them — arrive to speak before any ordinary channel of news could carry the same message.

## A War's Quiet Aftermath: Yugawara and the Wounded Officers

![A young Japanese woman, Keiko, in a traditional wooden bath, steam rising around her](/images/posts/the-girl-at-the-station-platform-a-meiji-era-mystery-of-a-death-that-came-before/shot_026.jpeg)
*A young Japanese woman, Keiko, in a traditional wooden bath, steam rising around her.*


To understand why two young women were traveling alone on the Tōkaidō line in February, one must understand what Yugawara had become. After Mukden, after the long casualty lists of 1905, the hot spring towns of Kanagawa Prefecture filled with recovering officers. Atami and Yugawara were considered dignified, proper places for a soldier's body to mend — not resorts of idleness, but sanctioned convalescence, quietly woven into the fabric of a nation absorbing its war.

**Mizusawa Keiko**'s cousin, Fujio, was one of these men, wounded at Mukden and sent home to heal. Though the family spoke of him as her elder brother, everyone understood the two were promised in marriage. Keiko had wanted for weeks to visit him during the school examination recess, but a young woman of her generation could not travel and lodge away from home unescorted. She needed a companion of good standing — and so she asked her friend, the girl who would one day sit across from Kidō and describe what happened next.

The witness's parents consented without difficulty. Two nights at the inn, a return on Tuesday morning — the arrangement was fixed and spoken aloud before either girl left Tokyo, a detail that matters, because everything that followed would measure itself against that promise.

## The Journey Out, and the Journey That Went Wrong

![Hakone Mountains](/images/posts/the-girl-at-the-station-platform-a-meiji-era-mystery-of-a-death-that-came-before/shot_038.jpeg)
*Hakone Mountains.*


They left from **Shimbashi station**, since Tokyo Station did not yet exist, boarding beneath soft, unseasonably warm February light. Past Kōzu, past Odawara, the sea kept appearing and vanishing beside the coastal hills, and the two girls grew giddy with the ordinary pleasure of being young and briefly loose from their households. A horse carriage carried them the final stretch into Yugawara, arriving near four in the afternoon, where Fujio met them with evident joy, already speaking of returning to Tokyo within the month.

Monday passed in the unhurried mood particular to a spring hot-spring town — a mood the witness said required no description, since anyone who has known one already understands its ease. Tuesday came gray, a thin mist blurring the camellias in the inn's courtyard. In the bath that morning, Keiko turned the weather over in her mind and asked whether her friend truly had to leave that day.

The witness held to her word. She had promised her parents two days, and Tuesday was the day she had given. Keiko considered sending a telegram home, blaming the rain, and the witness understood — without being told — that the rain was not the true obstacle. A young woman newly reunited with her betrothed does not wish to leave him after a single day. It was, she thought, entirely natural, and she held no resentment toward the thought.

But Keiko would not let her friend travel back unescorted. Apologizing again and again for cutting the visit short, she agreed to leave with her after all. Fujio and Keiko rode with the witness as far as the station, and Keiko, with a strange note of something like guilt in her voice, said she would return to Yugawara once the weather cleared.

## The Voice at the Gate

![The witness's hand tightly grasping a wooden post on the platform, her knuckles white](/images/posts/the-girl-at-the-station-platform-a-meiji-era-mystery-of-a-death-that-came-before/shot_050.jpeg)
*The witness's hand tightly grasping a wooden post on the platform, her knuckles white.*


The journey back unspooled in ordinary stages — the horse tramway to Odawara, the electric line to Kōzu, arrival a little after eleven. The sky had begun to break open, pale sun slipping between clouds, the air turning strangely warm for February. At a tea stall she bought pickled plums for her mother, then sat in the narrow second-class waiting room, oddly heavy-headed, a close warmth pressing at her temples she could not account for.

Near noon, the gate opened and the crowd surged toward the platform. It was there, pressed among strangers, that the voice found her — close, almost kind. She turned and saw a girl of perhaps fifteen or sixteen standing just to her left: pale-skinned, narrow-featured, her left eye clouded with a faint milky film, dressed in a padded cotton *kasuri* bound with an obi of red *meirinsu* wool. The girl looked directly at her and spoke again. *Keiko-san is dead.*

Almost without meaning to, the witness asked — has Keiko-san died? — and the girl, she was certain, answered only with a silent nod. Then the crowd closed between them, carrying the witness through the gate, and when she turned back there was no one there. She searched the platform, the yard beyond it. No face matching that clouded eye and red obi could be found anywhere in the station, though the girl had stood close enough to touch only a moment before.

She told herself it was nonsense — the rattle of an overcrowded gate, a misheard phrase, the fever still sitting behind her eyes. Keiko, laughing and well, had embraced her only hours earlier. There was no reasonable way a stranger could know such a thing, still less know it before it had happened at all. This is the hinge on which the whole kaidan turns, and Kidō recorded it without embellishment, exactly as the witness gave it to him.

## The Letter That Was Never Sent

The Tokyo train arrived, and the other passengers boarded around her. She did not move. Some reluctance she could not name held her on the platform until the train pulled away without her, and in its absence she found she no longer wished to go home at all. She turned back toward Odawara, toward Yugawara, toward the inn she had left that morning in perfect health and spirits.

Keiko was dead when she arrived. Kidō did not soften the record in the telling, and neither will this account. After the witness departed, Keiko had sent a telegram home asking to extend her stay because of the rain, and had sat afterward to write a letter addressed to the very friend who had just left her side.

Fujio went alone to bathe. When he returned, he found Keiko slumped forward over the low table and believed, at first, she was only lost in thought. She did not answer when he spoke. She did not stir when he touched her shoulder. The physician who came pronounced it failure of the heart, noting she had suffered a bout of *beriberi* the previous year — a detail offered without certainty of cause, only as a fact on record.

The letter lay finished inside its envelope, sealed, addressed, never sent — written in Keiko's own apologetic hand, meant to explain why she had chosen, after all, to stay one more day in the rain. No one at the inn had sent word to Kōzu. No messenger had gone ahead of the morning train. Fujio, when asked, could offer nothing; he had been too undone by what he found to think of anyone's errand but his own grief.

No girl answering to that pale face, that clouded eye, that red woolen obi, was ever identified by the household, the inn, or the station staff. The witness kept the sealed envelope, and the question inside it, for the rest of her life. The record notes only that she never received a satisfactory answer for who had spoken to her that morning, or how the words had reached her before the news itself ever could.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

Within the wider body of Japanese folklore, the figure of a stranger who delivers news of death before any living person could know it belongs to a recognizable, if rarely documented, category — sometimes folded into discussions of *shirei* or death-warning encounters, distinct from the vengeful apparitions more common to Edo period theater and print. Kidō's collection is valuable precisely because it resists sensationalism: no demon, no curse, no vengeful spirit, only an unexplained girl and a sentence spoken too early.

**Okamoto Kidō**'s broader body of ghost writing sits in the lineage that runs backward through **Ueda Akinari**'s *Ugetsu Monogatari* and **Asai Ryōi**'s *Otogi Bōko*, texts that first established *kaidan* as a literary form worth preserving rather than dismissing as rumor. Kidō's innovation was journalistic restraint — treating oral testimony the way Akinari treated classical sources, as material deserving careful transcription rather than embellishment.

Little remains today of the physical setting. The old Tōkaidō line stations at Kōzu and Odawara have been rebuilt many times over; Yugawara remains a hot spring town, its wartime convalescent inns long since replaced or renamed. What survives is the testimony itself, filed among Kidō's papers and now accessible through the Aozora Bunko digital archive, where readers can still find the witness's account in something close to her own words.

What the story leaves behind is not resolution but residue — a sealed letter never opened for its intended reader, a face no one could place, and a sentence that arrived before the event it described. *If the source didn't say it, neither do we* — and the source, in this case, says only that the words came, and came correctly, and that no one has ever explained how.
