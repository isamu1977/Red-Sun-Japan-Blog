The wind reaches the standing rocks before any traveler does. It comes down off the ridge in the late afternoon, scattering dry leaves across the mountain road, rattling through the gap where two great stones lean toward each other like the folding panels of a screen. A man walking home in that hour would feel it against his collar and think nothing of it — until the moment he did.

This is the shape in which the tale survives: a quiet road, a season's absence, a husband walking home with his wife's face fixed in his mind, and something waiting behind him that he never saw. It is one of the darker inheritances passed between the storytelling traditions of the Korean peninsula and the *kaidan* miscellanies compiled across the Edo period, where foreign ghost-records were absorbed, retitled, and set loose again among Japanese readers hungry for tales of betrayal answered from beyond the grave.

## The Scholar on the Mountain Road

![Distracted Study](/images/posts/the-evil-monk-a-wife-a-murder-and-a-vengeful-ghost/shot_012.jpeg)
*Distracted Study.*


The record names him **Seon-yang**, only son of a retired minister called **Jeong**, living in an unnamed hamlet south of the old capital. No precise year survives — only the custom around which the story turns. In the late Joseon period, a newly married *yangban* son was expected to leave his bride almost immediately, climbing to some mountain temple to read toward the state examinations that would determine the whole shape of his adult life. The separation was not cruelty. It was procedure, ordinary and unremarkable, the kind of domestic arrangement that appears in a hundred unremarked households and never once makes it into the historical record as anything but background.

It is this unremarkable custom that opens the door through which the horror enters.

Seon-yang was eighteen when his father arranged his marriage to a young woman the sources describe only as having "the appearance of a flower." He left her that same spring, as expected, for a bare room at a mountain temple, trying and failing to keep her face out of his reading. He did not go down to see her — that would have shamed his parents — so he wrote letters instead, entrusting them to a young monk whose quiet errand it ordinarily was to carry such things between husband and wife. He read her replies morning and night, the record says, "like a man rationing water."

By autumn he could bear the separation no longer. He invented a visit to his parents as cover and came down the mountain alone, imagining his wife's joy at the sight of him. He never arrived. At the standing rocks, where the path narrows between the leaning stones, something struck him from behind, and the road tilted, and the world went red and then dark. The village was told, afterward, that a tiger had taken him. It had not.

## A Marriage Unraveled in Silence

![Reading by Candlelight](/images/posts/the-evil-monk-a-wife-a-murder-and-a-vengeful-ghost/shot_023.jpeg)
*Reading by Candlelight.*


The monk who struck him down was the same one who had carried the letters — the same trusted go-between who had, in the months of Seon-yang's absence, made himself far more than a messenger in that household. The betrayal had been building quietly beneath the surface of ordinary correspondence, invisible to anyone reading only the words on the page. What the letters never recorded was the growing closeness between the wife and the man who delivered them, nor the understanding that eventually passed between the two of them on the road where her husband would walk home.

For three years afterward, the old minister grieved for a son he believed a tiger had carried off. His daughter-in-law continued to live beneath his roof, continued to receive his sympathy, continued — unknown to anyone — to receive the monk as well, in rooms lit by careful candlelight and emptied, each time, of witnesses. The record is explicit that the monk's conduct was judged, even by its own telling, "uglier even than hers." No further elaboration survives, only the moral weight of the phrase itself.

## The Archer at the Garden Wall

![A young man, a scholar in a muted blue robe, bowing formally by Yi-jang's bedside](/images/posts/the-evil-monk-a-wife-a-murder-and-a-vengeful-ghost/shot_033.jpeg)
*A young man, a scholar in a muted blue robe, bowing formally by Yi-jang's bedside.*


The second half of the tale belongs to a stranger entirely — a young examination candidate named **Yi-jang**, walking home from the archery grounds at the *Mohwagwan* one evening when a passing palanquin, its fittings trimmed in gold, caught a sudden whirlwind that pulled its curtain briefly aside. He saw, for a moment, a woman of striking beauty, and something in him — he could never afterward explain what — would not let the matter rest. He followed the palanquin at a distance through the copper light of early evening until it passed through the gate of a large estate on the edge of town, and then he did not leave.

From a rise beside the back wall, Yi-jang watched candlelight move behind two sets of windows deep into the night. He saw the woman read aloud to an older woman — her mother-in-law, by every appearance — until she was sent, with perfect grace, to finish the tale tomorrow. He saw her retire alone to a second chamber and sit listening, as he assumed, for a husband's returning footsteps. He felt, absurdly, a flicker of envy for a man he had never met.

The footsteps that came were not a husband's. A shape arrived at the garden door, knocked, and was let in from within. What Yi-jang witnessed afterward through that window curdled his earlier envy into something closer to disgust — the woman was not waiting for any husband. She was waiting for a monk, and the warmth between them, lit by a single lamp, spoke of long and practiced intimacy rather than a single night's indiscretion.

What happened next, the record keeps mercifully brief. Yi-jang, hidden in a stand of bamboo, nocked an arrow he had not quite decided to loose — and loosed it anyway. The lamp inside seemed to gutter in that same instant, and a weight fell, and a stillness settled over the room that even Yi-jang, from his place in the dark, understood he had no business witnessing further. He withdrew the way he had come and told no one what he had done.

## The Ghost at the Pillow

![Discovery of Family Relics](/images/posts/the-evil-monk-a-wife-a-murder-and-a-vengeful-ghost/shot_044.jpeg)
*Discovery of Family Relics.*


That night, behind shutters closed fast from the inside, Yi-jang woke to find a slight young scholar in a blue robe standing at his pillow — though no door had opened and no one could have entered. The visitor bowed with careful formality and gave his name: **Seon-yang**, only son of Minister Jeong. He had come, he said, in gratitude. Through Yi-jang's hand, that night, his enemy had finally answered for what he'd done.

He told the rest himself, in a voice that seemed to cost him some effort to produce. Three years before, newly married, he had gone up the mountain to study as custom demanded, and the monk entrusted with his letters had turned his wife against him instead — the two of them waiting, in the end, for his return on the very road where he was struck down and hidden in a stone hollow, the village left to believe a tiger responsible. His father had grieved a false story for three years. His wife had gone on living beneath that same roof, the monk beneath it with her.

The ghost asked one thing only: that Yi-jang find his father, lead him to the body in the stone hollow, and see it properly buried — and only then turn to the matter of his wife. The monk's body, he said, still lay wrapped in cloth beneath the floorboards of her room, proof enough if proof were required. Before Yi-jang could answer, the vision dissolved, and the locked room was simply a room again.

## The Blue Bird and the Stone Hollow

In the morning Yi-jang went to the house he had watched the night before and learned, for the first time, whose house it was. He told the old minister he knew where his son's remains could be found and asked to be taken there before he said anything further. They rode out together along the mountain road, and below the temple path, where the standing rocks leaned over the way, a small blue bird settled on a stone ledge and called — three clear notes, over and over — refusing to stop until Yi-jang followed it to a narrow crevice choked with thorn.

Inside lay bones still wrapped in the rotted remains of travelling clothes. The old minister knelt and wept before a single word was spoken, recognizing his son's robe by sight alone.

Back at the house, Minister Jeong asked his daughter-in-law's permission to dig beneath her floor for a store of jade he claimed to have hidden there years before. She grew flustered, asked him to wait while she cleared the room, and when he did not wait, she bolted for the door — where Yi-jang's hand was already closing around it. Beneath the floorboards lay the monk's body, the cloth around it long since darkened with old, dried stillness.

Her father and brother were sent for, and before them she confessed everything plainly, without concealment, as though some tension long held inside her had simply given way at last. Her own father drew the blade at his waist. The record says nothing further of that room — only that the household's long deception ended there, in silence, and that no one present spoke of it again.

That night Seon-yang came once more to Yi-jang's pillow, calmer now, almost at ease, to recite an examination passage twice over and urge him to commit it to memory. Yi-jang did. He passed. He rose, in time, to high office, under the quiet and steady patronage of a minister who had buried his son twice — once beneath a story about a tiger, and once, properly, on the mountain where a blue bird had sung and would not be silent until it was heard.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

No Japanese kaidan collector claims this tale as a native invention, and honesty requires saying so plainly. Its roots lie in Korean Joseon-era tale collections, where tiger-cover stories, examination-bound husbands, and ghosts returning to settle unfinished domestic business are a recognizable and recurring pattern. What survives in the Japanese tradition is the *transmission* — the way such foreign ghost-records were gathered, translated, and folded into miscellanies like *Konjaku Kaidan* alongside native material, in the same spirit that later produced **Asai Ryōi's** *Otogi Bōko* (1666) and, a century on, **Ueda Akinari's** *Ugetsu Monogatari*, both of which freely absorbed continental ghost-lore into distinctly Japanese literary frames.

Readers of **Lafcadio Hearn** will recognize the pattern even without the Korean setting: a wronged spirit who cannot rest until a living stranger completes an unfinished obligation, a household secret hidden beneath floorboards rather than confessed aloud, a natural creature — bird, moth, cricket — acting as the dead's last instrument in the world of the living. Hearn's own retellings, and the scholarly apparatus built around them in the early twentieth century, treat such border-crossing tales as evidence of a shared East Asian grammar of grief and restitution, rather than isolated regional inventions.

No stone marker records the standing rocks of this story, and no temple claims the ruined room beneath the floorboards as a pilgrimage site. What remains is textual — manuscript lineage, translation choices, the quiet decision by some Edo-period compiler to keep the tale's Korean geography intact rather than relocate it to a more familiar province. That restraint is itself a kind of archival honesty, the same honesty this companion piece has tried to preserve: if the record does not know where the bird went, neither should we pretend to.
