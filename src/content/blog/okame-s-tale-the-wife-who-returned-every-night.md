A temple bell on the edge of Tosa marked the hour just before dawn, and in one house that bell was the only thing that could make a dead woman let go of a living man. For months the household had organized its nights around that sound without ever naming why. A mother listening at a sliding door heard two voices instead of one.

A son grew thinner by the week, the way a leaf loses its color in the eighth month — not all at once, but so slowly that one morning the branch is simply bare. This is the account preserved from old **Tosa** province, on the island of Shikoku, and it survives today as one of the quieter, more disciplined entries in the *kaidan* tradition: a ghost story told, its collectors insisted, not as invention but as something that happened.

## The Bell at the Edge of Tosa

![Couple's Serene Interaction](/images/posts/okame-s-tale-the-wife-who-returned-every-night/shot_012.jpeg)
*Couple's Serene Interaction.*


The household belonged to a *chōja* — a man of property and standing — named **Gon'emon**. His daughter, called **Okame** in the record, had married a young man named **Hachiemon**, and for two years the marriage drew no complaint from either side. The source is unusually careful on this point: Okame loved her husband visibly, almost conspicuously, enough that neighbors assumed her the jealous type.

But the text insists Hachiemon gave her no cause for jealousy, ever, and that no harsh word passed between them. This detail is not decoration. It is the floor the rest of the story is built on, because a marriage without friction is precisely what makes the horror that follows so difficult to look at directly.

Within two years, Okame fell ill with a wasting disease then moving through Tosa, one that no physician's skill could turn back. Those afflicted could not eat or drink. They slid into a shallow half-sleep and were, in the record's own phrasing, "troubled by strange dreams." Okame declined under constant nursing until she understood, before anyone told her outright, that she would not recover.

## A Promise Asked at the Deathbed

![Okame's Last Breath](/images/posts/okame-s-tale-the-wife-who-returned-every-night/shot_022.jpeg)
*Okame's Last Breath.*


She called Hachiemon to her side. She told him she could not find words for his kindness through her illness, that no better husband existed. She was not yet twenty-five. She had seen in her mirror that very morning that she would die before the day ended, and she did not want comfort — she wanted one promise. When she was gone, she said, the family would press him to remarry. She asked him to refuse them. She asked him never to take another wife.

Hachiemon answered quickly, almost lightly, the way people answer when a request sounds smaller than the weight pressing behind it. "Is that all? It is nothing. I promise." Okame rose half up from the bed. "Ah — I am happy," she said. Then she fell back, and in that same instant her breath stopped. The scene reads, in the surviving account, less like melodrama than like testimony — spare, exact, unwilling to embellish a moment that needed none.

## The Debt That Outlived the Grave

![Misty Graveyard](/images/posts/okame-s-tale-the-wife-who-returned-every-night/shot_032.jpeg)
*Misty Graveyard.*


Grief was the first explanation the village reached for when Hachiemon began to fail after the funeral. A devoted widower losing color and weight seemed, at first, like ordinary mourning taken to an unusual depth. But the months did not stop, and neither did the decline. Physicians examined him and agreed on only one thing: whatever afflicted him was not an ordinary illness. It seemed to rise from some disturbance of the mind — grief, perhaps, working on the body from the inside, the way such afflictions were commonly understood in this strand of **Japanese folklore**.

His parents asked him directly and received nothing. He insisted there was no hidden sorrow. When they suggested he remarry, as grieving sons were expected to eventually agree to, he refused with a stubbornness that unsettled them more than it reassured them. A promise made to the dead, he said, could not be broken. The household kept watching him thin toward something they did not yet have a word for.

It was his mother, finally, who broke the silence — not through accusation but through grief she could no longer hold back, weeping and pleading until whatever had kept her son quiet gave way. What he told her is the hinge the entire tale turns on, and the record sets it down almost verbatim.

### What Hachiemon Confessed

"Okame cannot find peace in the other world," he told her. "No memorial service has helped her. It seems I must travel that road with her, or she will never rest." He said she had come back every single night since the funeral, lying beside him as she had in life, speaking so softly that no one else could catch the words — and always telling him to keep her visits secret. "Sometimes I think she cannot truly be dead at all," he said. "Her manner, her ways, are exactly as they were." She stayed until the temple bell sounded near dawn. Then she was gone.

He added one more thing, almost as an afterthought that was not an afterthought at all: he believed she wanted him to die as well, and if the choice were only his, he would not mind going. It was only the knowledge that his body belonged first to his parents that had finally loosened his tongue.

## Opening the Grave

![Several men, their faces grim and strained, carefully lifting a heavy wooden coffin from the earth](/images/posts/okame-s-tale-the-wife-who-returned-every-night/shot_042.jpeg)
*Several men, their faces grim and strained, carefully lifting a heavy wooden coffin from the earth.*


The mother went straight to the family temple. The priest, long experienced in such matters, showed no surprise at what she told him — this was not, he said, the first case of its kind, and Hachiemon could still be saved, but only if they moved at once. He could already see the shadow of death settling over the young man's face. Relatives from both families were summoned to the temple without Hachiemon's knowledge. Okame's grave would have to be opened.

What they found inside the coffin stopped the breath of everyone standing in that graveyard. Okame sat in the formal burial posture, her face unmarked by months underground, wearing — the record is specific here — a faint smile. There was no visible sign of death on her at all. When the priest ordered her lifted from the coffin, the fear sharpened into something closer to horror: her body was warm, and it yielded like living flesh, though she had been buried for months.

The priest took up his brush and inscribed sacred Sanskrit characters — *bonji* — of protective merit across her forehead, chest, hands, and feet. Before she was returned to the earth, he performed the *segaki* rite on her behalf, the ritual feeding of hungry ghosts meant to release a spirit still bound to the living world. She did not come again. Hachiemon's strength returned by slow degrees, and the village that had quietly begun preparing a second funeral watched the color return instead to a face they had already given up on.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

The tale now circulated in English under the title **Okame's Tale: The Wife Who Returned Every Night** belongs to a specific corner of the *kaidan* genre: the regional anecdote, gathered from local informants rather than composed for literary effect. **Lafcadio Hearn**, the Greek-Irish writer who settled in Japan and devoted much of his career to recording exactly these kinds of accounts, preserved this one from a Japanese-language source whose author he notes pointedly declined to resolve the story's final question — whether Hachiemon ever kept, or broke, his promise.

That withheld ending is characteristic of the tradition Hearn worked within. Unlike the more ornamented ghost literature of writers such as **Ueda Akinari**, whose *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776) shaped supernatural encounter into moral parable, or **Asai Ryōi**, whose earlier *Otogi Bōko* (1666) helped establish the vocabulary of Japanese ghost fiction for a popular readership, this Tosa account reads closer to deposition than to designed literature.

Though no year is given, the story's texture — the household structure, the role of the Buddhist temple as the final authority, the *segaki* rite performed over an exhumed body — is unmistakably rooted in the social and religious fabric of the **Edo period**, when temple registries, ancestral memorial obligations, and a settled vocabulary for distinguishing restless spirits from peaceful ones were already deeply embedded in rural life. The warm, unspoiled corpse is a recurring motif across East Asian ghost literature, typically read as the sign of a *goryō* or unsettled spirit refusing to complete its passage — not a vampiric threat, but a grief so total it has not yet learned how to end.

No temple registry, grave marker, or regional record identifying the real Gon'emon household in Tosa has surfaced in modern archives; the account survives only through the nineteenth-century collection that preserved it, and like much of this *kaidan* tradition, its historical kernel is inseparable from its telling. What remains is the structure itself — a promise extracted at a deathbed, a debt the living cannot see how to pay, and a silence the original author refused to fill. The refusal is the point. Some stories end. This one simply stops, at the exact moment the bell sounds.
