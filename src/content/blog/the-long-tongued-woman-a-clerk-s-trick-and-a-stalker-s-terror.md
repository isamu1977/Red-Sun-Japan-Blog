The grass along that suburban path has long since been paved over, but the record of what stood among its foxtail weed and amaranth survives — printed cheaply, sold at a railway kiosk, read once by a commuter and then, improbably, read again by us nearly a century later. This is the companion essay to **the long tongued woman a clerk s trick and a stalker s terror**, the tale of a young clerk named Kikue, a length of pale root-cake pressed between her lips in the dark, and a man who would not stop following women long after that night was over.

## The Shinkai-Machi: A New Kind of Haunted Ground

![Glowing Cafe on Corner](/images/posts/the-long-tongued-woman-a-clerk-s-trick-and-a-stalker-s-terror/shot_011.jpeg)
*Glowing Cafe on Corner.*


The setting matters as much as the scare. The account comes down through one of the slim, cheaply printed *kaidan* anthologies that circulated Tokyo's expanding suburbs in the early Shōwa years — digest-sized volumes bearing titles like "Strange Occurrences of the New Residential Towns," compiled from letters sent in by readers who lived along the new streetcar lines. These lines, built through what had been rice paddy and farmland only a decade earlier, created a peculiar psychic geography: half-built lots beside finished houses, electric poles painted municipal green standing in fields that had not yet forgotten what they were.

This liminal landscape — the *shinkai-machi*, or "newly opened town" — is itself a kind of folklore engine. Japanese ghost tradition has always favored thresholds: the bridge, the well, the crossroads, the boundary between rice field and forest. The early twentieth-century suburb simply manufactured new thresholds faster than the old stories could fill them. An unfinished house with tiled roof but open walls, as in this account, is architecturally a liminal space — neither inside nor outside, neither home nor ruin — and it is precisely there that Kikue takes her stand against whatever follows her.

## A Clerk's Walk Home

![Terrified Eyes of the Hunted](/images/posts/the-long-tongued-woman-a-clerk-s-trick-and-a-stalker-s-terror/shot_020.jpeg)
*Terrified Eyes of the Hunted.*


Kikue was, by the plain terms of the record, unremarkable — a company clerk who walked the same route every morning and evening without a second thought. The night in question was different only because her mother lay ill, needing warm *konnyaku*, the pale jellied cake pressed from devil's-tongue root and laid hot against the skin for stomach pain. Her father was in Sendai on business. So Kikue went out past ten o'clock, alone, to the grocer's, and came home with three blocks of it wrapped in her handkerchief.

The early portion of her walk is almost domestic in its texture — two drunk young men she barely notices, a woman with two boys, a café glowing on the corner where she imagines, with a private flutter, that a certain young man from her office might walk her home if he knew she were out this late. This daydream is not incidental. It is the ordinary interior life of a young woman on an ordinary night, rendered by the source with a deliberate plainness, so that what follows lands with the full shock of intrusion.

## The Thing That Matched Her Pace

![Deserted Alley at Night](/images/posts/the-long-tongued-woman-a-clerk-s-trick-and-a-stalker-s-terror/shot_029.jpeg)
*Deserted Alley at Night.*


At the three-way fork, the record changes register. One road ran lit and graveled past the streetcar stop. The other — her habitual shortcut — ran dark through grass lots, past a foreign-style house where a girl sometimes played with a small dog in the evenings. Kikue took the shortcut, as always, telling herself the moon gave light enough. It was here, among the green-painted utility poles, that she first sensed — without proof, without sound — that she was no longer alone.

She tested it, as any careful mind might: climbing a low red-earth bank planted with chinquapin trees, reasoning that an honest traveler walking home would have no cause to follow her off the path. He climbed it. She descended the far side and glanced back — he was already at the lip of the slope, exactly where she had stood a moment before. The record is spare here, almost clinical, and the effect is worse for it. There is no mistaking a hunter once he has shown you his pace.

Kikue made a choice that the source treats, quietly, as the decisive moment of the whole account: she did not scream. She reasoned, in the span of a few running steps, that a cry in that dead hour might summon no one — and might only provoke a faster strike. Instead she ran for the skeleton of an unfinished house standing roofless in the weeds, tiled but open-walled, and vanished into the black of its doorway.

## What Waited in the Unfinished House

![Figures in Narrow Lane](/images/posts/the-long-tongued-woman-a-clerk-s-trick-and-a-stalker-s-terror/shot_038.jpeg)
*Figures in Narrow Lane.*


What the man found when he reached that threshold is recorded without ornament, because — as the source itself seems to understand — none is needed. Moonlight fell thin across the earthen floor, against a post still raw with the smell of new-cut wood. There stood a woman with a narrow, pale face, and from her open mouth hung something six or seven inches long, limp and faintly glistening, swaying as though it breathed on its own.

The trick, we later learn, was a strip of wet gray *konnyaku* held between her lips — the very medicine she had walked out to buy for her ailing mother, repurposed in an instant as a weapon against the dark. The man did not stay to study it. He cried out once and fled as though the ground had opened beneath him. Kikue, for her part, walked home with her parcel intact, telling no one for months.

## Masao's Long Night

The man had a name — **Masao** — and a history the record does not flinch from. He had once driven automobiles for hire, until a night he struck a pedestrian and, rather than bring the injured man to a hospital, had him beaten and abandoned before speeding off. His license was revoked; he drifted to the new town, lodging above a general store, nominally hunting work as a mechanic's assistant, and, in hours the source neither dwells on nor conceals, hunting women after dark.

That same night, before Kikue, he had already tried and failed once in a neighboring town, scared off by a passerby, and had gone drinking to steady his nerve before trying again. This detail matters: the apparition did not create a predator. It interrupted one already in motion, already practiced, already indifferent to the harm of his earlier crime. The horror the tongue delivers is retributive before it is supernatural — it arrives, in the story's own quiet logic, for a man already guilty of something worse than fright.

His unraveling afterward is drawn with unusual psychological patience for a folk pamphlet. He burst into his lodgings bolting the shutter, unable to climb his own stairs without the landlady going ahead to light the lamp. The landlord, suspicious of his tenant's record, asked outright whether he had struck someone with a car again. Masao denied it weakly and buried himself under the bedding for ten days, "emptied out," the tongue burning behind his eyes.

## The Second Tongue

Recovery, when it came, looked almost like redemption. Masao found honest work with a new omnibus line, began eating his evenings at the café instead of alone, drank a little, stayed out later — no longer so afraid of the dark. The reader, and perhaps the original audience of the pamphlet, might have hoped the story was closing toward an ordinary moral: fright corrects the wicked man.

It does not. Weeks later, at a night fair strung with lantern-lit stalls, Masao let his old hunger lead him again — a hand laid against a girl's *obi*, her fingers answering his, the lane between a vacant lot and the backs of houses, a grove of sawtooth oak, a new stone *torii* with a single bulb burning above it. He drew close. He spoke to her about how quiet the place was. And in that small circle of electric light, the narrow face turned fully toward him, and the tongue came down again — exactly as before, exactly as long, exactly as pale.

He ran until police found him raving in the streets and delivered him to the charity ward of the asylum. The record offers no bridge between the first tongue, which we know to have been Kikue's trick with the *konnyaku*, and the second, which appears in a different town, before a different stranger, with no clerk and no root-cake anywhere near it. This unexplained repetition is, by design or by accident of folk transmission, the story's true and lingering horror.

## Historical Legacy & Archival Footnotes

Japan's folk catalogue already contained women with unnatural tongues long before this Shōwa-era pamphlet went to print — the swamp-dwelling **Nure-onna**, chronicled in Edo-period picture scrolls and later catalogued by **Toriyama Sekien** in his *yōkai* compendiums, and the elongating-necked **rokurokubi**, a staple of Edo kibyōshi and later Meiji storytelling, both traffic in the same unease: a woman's mouth, ordinarily a vessel of speech and intimacy, becomes the site of something inhuman. This account inherits that visual grammar directly, even as it relocates the horror from forest swamp to electric-lit suburb.

What makes this particular tale unusual within the *kaidan* tradition is its refusal to resolve the ambiguity it creates. Classic Edo-period ghost literature — the moralizing tales collected by **Asai Ryōi** in *Otogi Bōko* (1666), or the elegant tragedies of **Ueda Akinari**'s *Ugetsu Monogatari* (1776) — typically closes its supernatural loop with explanation, punishment, or release. Here, the pamphlet offers a rational cause for the first apparition and then, without comment, allows an identical apparition to recur under conditions where no rational cause exists. The silence is the point.

Few physical traces of the original *shinkai-machi* survive identifiably today; Japan's early Shōwa streetcar suburbs have long since been absorbed into the undifferentiated sprawl of greater Tokyo, their chinquapin banks and unfinished tile-roofed lots paved into ordinary residential blocks decades ago. What remains is textual — the pamphlet itself, one of thousands of such reader-submitted volumes that scholars of Japanese popular print culture now treat as valuable, if unglamorous, folklore archives, preserving how ordinary commuters narrated fear to one another in the years before postwar reconstruction erased the landscape that produced it.

The philosophical residue of the tale outlasts its geography. **Lafcadio Hearn**, writing decades earlier in *Kwaidan* (1904), argued that Japanese ghost stories derive their lasting power not from the apparition itself but from the moral debt it reveals — a debt the living carry whether or not they ever learn its source. Masao's second tongue, appearing without Kikue, without *konnyaku*, without any human hand behind it, reads precisely as that kind of debt: not a trick repeated, but a reckoning that trick merely interrupted once, and could not stop twice.

### Primary Texts & Scholarly References

- Asai Ryōi (1666), *Otogi Bōko* — foundational Edo-period kaidan anthology establishing the moral architecture of Japanese ghost narrative.
- Ueda Akinari (1776), *Ugetsu Monogatari* — classical model for ambiguous, unresolved supernatural encounter in Japanese literature.
