During the Heian and Muromachi periods, when darkness fell over the capital of Heian-kyo (modern Kyoto), the streets ceased to belong to mortal men. According to aristocratic diaries and Buddhist chronicles, on specific nights dictated by the Chinese zodiac calendar, the veil between the physical realm and the underworld disintegrated, unleashing the **Hyakki Yagyo**—the Night Parade of One Hundred Demons.

Led by the demonic warlord Nurarihyon, the procession featured an unearthly host of supernatural entities: grotesque *oni*, shapeshifting *kitsune*, and animate household tools possessed by spirits after reaching one hundred years of age (*tsukumogami*). To look upon the parade was considered fatal; legends held that anyone who accidentally witnessed the procession would be struck dead or spirited away into eternal damnation.

Aristocrats, courtiers, and samurai alike took solemn precautions. On recorded parade nights, households sealed their lattice shutters, drew heavy paper screens, and chanted protective Shingon sutras until dawn broke. Those forced to travel carried written talismans (*ofuda*) consecrated by the imperial Onmyoji masters to shield themselves from spectral retribution.

The iconography of the Night Parade was immortalized in famous picture scrolls (*emaki*), most notably the 16th-century masterpiece attributed to Tosa Mitsunobu. Today, the legend stands as one of the most evocative expressions of Japan's dark folkloric imagination—a testament to an ancient era where the shadows of night concealed terrors far beyond mortal comprehension.
